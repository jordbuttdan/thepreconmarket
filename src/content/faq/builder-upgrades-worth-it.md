The décor appointment is where a disciplined budget usually dies. A simple rule sorts most of it.

**Buy what you cannot add later. Think hard about everything else.**

## Usually worth it — structural and hard to retrofit

These require access to framing, concrete or rough plumbing, and doing them afterward is disruptive, expensive or impossible:

- **Basement bathroom rough-in.** Cheap now, a concrete-cutting job later. Almost always worth it.
- **Extra or enlarged basement windows**, and an **egress window** if you may ever finish a bedroom down there.
- **A side or basement entrance**, where the lot and zoning permit — decisive if a secondary suite is ever a possibility.
- **Raised ceiling heights**, main floor or basement. Cannot be changed afterward and materially affects how the house feels.
- **Structural changes** — removing a wall, extending a room, a larger opening, an upgraded staircase location.
- **Electrical rough-ins** in finished-ceiling locations: pot lights, pendant locations, EV charger circuit, data runs.
- **Gas lines** to the kitchen, the deck, the garage.
- **Rough-ins for central vac or a future hot tub.**

## Usually not worth builder pricing — cosmetic

Builder margins on finishes are substantial, and the same work after closing is frequently far cheaper:

- **Flooring upgrades.** Often two to three times the retail-plus-install cost.
- **Kitchen cabinets and counters.** A full post-closing kitchen upgrade sometimes costs less than the builder's upgrade *allowance*.
- **Backsplashes and tile.**
- **Light fixtures.** Trivially replaced.
- **Paint colours.**
- **Appliance packages** — compare against a retail sale price before accepting.

The caveat: doing cosmetic work after closing means living through it, or delaying your move. Some buyers reasonably pay the premium to be done. That is a legitimate choice — just make it knowingly rather than because the décor consultant framed it as necessary.

## The price-band trap

Upgrades add to your purchase price, and price determines your rebate band. $40,000 of upgrades on a $1,470,000 home pushes you past **$1,500,000**, where the [federal first-time buyer GST rebate becomes zero](/faq/hst-rebate-over-1-5-million).

How upgrades count toward consideration depends on the documentation in your agreement — confirm with your lawyer and accountant. But ask **before** the décor appointment, not after you have signed selections.

## Practical rules

1. **Set a number before you walk in**, and treat it as a limit.
2. **Deal with rough-ins first**, then spend what is left on cosmetics if anything remains.
3. **Get the itemised price list in advance** if the builder will provide it.
4. **Price two or three cosmetic items externally** to calibrate the markup.
5. **Get every upgrade in writing** on the selection sheet — then verify each one individually at the [pre-delivery inspection](/faq/pre-delivery-inspection-pdi).
6. **Remember upgrades are usually non-refundable** and paid separately from the deposit schedule.
