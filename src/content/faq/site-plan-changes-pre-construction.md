Treat the site plan in the sales centre as an intention, not a promise. The homes get built. The things around them are approved separately, by different bodies, on their own timelines, and some of them are not the builder's to deliver at all.

## What you are actually looking at

That coloured plan on the wall is a **draft plan of subdivision**. In Ontario, a subdivision goes through two stages under section 51 of the Planning Act: **draft approval**, which comes with a list of conditions, and then **final approval and registration**, which is when the plan becomes real in law.

The gap between those two stages is where things move. Conditions of draft approval can be revised before final approval, blocks get reconfigured, and phasing shifts. A plan you were shown in 2026 can register differently in 2029.

## The three things buyers assume, ranked by how safe the assumption is

**Parkland is the safest.** Municipalities take parkland dedication as a condition of approval, under section 51.1 of the Planning Act, and the park block is conveyed to the town. The park is likely to exist. *When* it gets built, graded, planted and equipped is a separate question, and it is usually years after the first residents move in.

**Trails, stormwater ponds and road connections are middling.** They are engineering conditions in the subdivision agreement. They generally happen, often later than shown, and sometimes in a different alignment.

**A school block is the least safe, and it is the one buyers bank on most.** A builder can set aside land and label it "proposed school". Only the **school board** decides whether to acquire that block and build on it, and boards make that call on enrolment projections and their own capital funding. Blocks sit empty for a decade. Some are eventually released and sold for housing. If your children are the reason you are buying in a community, verify with the board directly, not with the sales centre.

## What to do about it

- **Ask for the registered plan**, or the draft plan with its conditions, rather than the marketing rendering. Your lawyer can obtain and read it.
- **Ask the school board**, by name of the block, what its intentions are. Ask in writing.
- **Check what the agreement says about changes.** Builder agreements routinely reserve the right to alter lot sizes, grades, elevations and the configuration of surrounding blocks. [What's in the agreement](/faq/what-is-in-agreement-of-purchase-and-sale-precon)
- **Do not pay a lot premium for an adjacency that is not registered.** A premium for backing onto "future park" is a premium for something that has not been approved in final form. [Lot premiums](/faq/lot-premiums-explained)
- **Discount the amenity timeline in your own head.** Retail, transit and services arrive after the homes, not with them.

## The honest version

New low-rise communities are car-dependent at launch almost without exception, and most of what makes an area convenient shows up years later. That is not a reason to avoid them, plenty of excellent neighbourhoods started exactly this way. It is a reason to buy the home and the location on what is there now, and treat the rest as upside rather than as something you have paid for.
