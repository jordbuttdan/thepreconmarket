No. This is the most important sentence on this website: **there is no statutory cooling-off period when you buy a freehold home in Ontario.**

Not ten days. Not three days. None.

## Where the confusion comes from

Ontario's Condominium Act gives condominium purchasers a **10-day rescission period**, a genuine statutory right to walk away, no reason required, deposit returned. It is well known, widely discussed, and it is the thing most buyers have heard about.

It does not apply to freehold homes. There is no equivalent provision for a detached home, a semi, or a freehold townhome. When you sign a builder's agreement of purchase and sale for a freehold home and the builder accepts it, you are bound.

## What "bound" actually means

- Your deposit is committed, on the schedule in the agreement.
- You cannot change your mind because rates moved, or your circumstances changed, or you saw a better home next weekend.
- If you fail to close, you can lose your deposit and face further exposure. [More on that](/faq/deposit-lost-if-cannot-close).

The only ways out are the ones written into the agreement, a financing condition if you negotiated one, a specific builder default, or a right the agreement itself grants you. Not a general right to reconsider.

## The partial exception: freehold with a POTL

Some freehold low-rise homes come with a **POTL**, a parcel of tied land attached to a common elements condominium corporation, which is how shared private roads, visitor parking or park blocks get maintained. [More on POTLs](/faq/potl-common-elements-condo-fees).

Where your purchase includes an interest in a common elements condominium corporation from the declarant, a rescission right **may** be engaged. This is genuinely nuanced and turns on how your specific transaction is structured and documented. It is not something to assume either way from a web page. If your agreement mentions a POTL or a common elements condominium corporation, ask your lawyer directly and promptly, because if a right exists it is measured in days.

## What to do instead of relying on a cooling-off period

Since you cannot undo the signature, the review has to happen **before** it:

1. **Have a real estate lawyer read the agreement before you sign.** Not after. Builder agreements are long, heavily amended, and drafted for the builder.
2. **Understand the deposit schedule** and whether you can actually fund it.
3. **Know your [rebate position](/faq/builder-keeps-hst-rebate-assignment)**, an HST clause can be an $80,000 contingent liability.
4. **Ask what is not included** in the base price.

Builders sell low-rise in timed releases precisely because urgency works. The pressure is real, but so is the fact that you are signing something irreversible. An hour with a lawyer beforehand is the cheapest insurance in this entire process.
