In low-rise, the lot is the one decision you cannot revisit. You can replace flooring, redo a kitchen, finish a basement. You cannot move the house, change the grade or remove the townhouse block that gets built behind you.

Most buyers spend more time choosing a backsplash.

## Start with the plan of subdivision

Ask for the **plan of subdivision**, not the marketing site plan. The marketing version shows your community; the plan of subdivision shows what is zoned around it.

Then ask, specifically:

- **What is behind this lot?** Not what it looks like now, what is *zoned*. "Backing onto green space" sometimes means backing onto a block zoned for future medium density.
- **Is the park built, funded and in the plan,** or aspirational?
- **Where do the future phases go,** and what types of homes?
- **Where is the stormwater management pond,** and what is its setback?
- **Which streets are collectors?** A collector road carries traffic a local street does not.

## What is worth paying for

**A walkout or lookout basement.** The strongest premium on most price lists. It changes what the house *is*: natural light, a usable lower level, an accessible rear yard, and potentially a legal secondary suite where zoning permits. It cannot be added later at any price. Frequently the best value on offer.

**Protected land behind you.** Ravine, conservation, a registered park block, a school site. Permanently no rear neighbour, and resale buyers pay for it because it cannot be manufactured.

**Meaningfully wider frontage.** 36 to 42 feet changes the house that fits on the lot. The difference is in the floor plan, not just the grass.

**A quiet position**, interior street, away from the collector, not backing a pond or a visitor lot.

## What usually is not

**Small frontage steps.** 30 to 32 feet. You are buying two feet of side yard.

**Corner lots.** Genuinely divisive. More light and a side yard, but more sidewalk to clear, more exposure, often more restrictive siting, and some resale buyers discount them. Pay a premium for one only if you actively want it.

**Pie-shaped lots** where the extra area is narrow, sloped or unusable.

**A "future park"** with no commitment behind it.

**Backing onto a stormwater pond.** Views and wildlife, also mosquitoes, fencing requirements and a maintenance easement. Depends entirely on the specific pond.

## The grading question

Grading is engineered across the whole block, and your lot's grade determines whether you can have a walkout, how many steps to your back door, and where water goes.

Ask for the **lot grading plan**. Look for: a rear yard that slopes away from the house, whether a swale crosses your lot, how many risers to the rear door, and whether a retaining wall is involved, and if so, whose it is to maintain.

And know that [sod, final grading and the driveway come after you move in](/faq/grading-sodding-driveway-timeline), sometimes a season or two later. Do not landscape before final approval; it can cost you both your deposit and your grading warranty.

## The tax trap

Lot premiums add to your purchase price, and your price sets your rebate band.

A $1,450,000 base price with a $60,000 ravine premium is a **$1,510,000** purchase, across the **$1,500,000 federal cliff**, where the first-time buyer GST rebate becomes **zero**. The premium can cost more than it costs. [The cliffs](/faq/hst-rebate-over-1-5-million).

Exactly how premiums count toward consideration depends on your agreement's documentation, a question for your lawyer and accountant. But ask it **before** you select the lot.

## Be ready when the release happens

Lots are allocated in order. The practical consequence is that preparation beats preference: financing pre-qualified, deposit funds liquid, priorities decided, lawyer briefed. [Platinum access](/faq/what-is-platinum-access-pre-construction) is only worth having if you can act on it.

Decide in advance, in writing, your top three lot choices and what you will not compromise on. Then you choose in sixty seconds instead of losing the lot while you think.
